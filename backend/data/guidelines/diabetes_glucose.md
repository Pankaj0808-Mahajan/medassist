# Glucose basics (educational summary - add the full ADA Standards of Care PDF to this folder)

Low blood glucose (hypoglycemia) is a glucose below 70 mg/dL (3.9 mmol/L). Below 54 mg/dL (3.0 mmol/L) it is clinically significant and needs prompt action. Symptoms include shakiness, sweating, hunger, fast heartbeat, confusion and, in severe cases, loss of consciousness. For a mild low in a person who is awake and able to swallow, the usual approach is the 15-15 rule: take about 15 grams of fast-acting carbohydrate such as glucose tablets or juice, recheck after 15 minutes and repeat if still low. A severe low, where the person cannot help themselves, is an emergency.

High blood glucose (hyperglycemia) is common in diabetes. Many adults with diabetes aim for 80-130 mg/dL before meals and below 180 mg/dL one to two hours after meals, and aim to spend most of the day (often more than 70 percent) between 70 and 180 mg/dL. Persistently high readings above 250 mg/dL, especially with nausea, vomiting, stomach pain, fast breathing or fruity-smelling breath, can signal a dangerous complication and need urgent medical advice. People with type 1 diabetes are often advised to check ketones when glucose stays above 250 mg/dL or when ill.

Diagnosis of diabetes is made by a clinician using an HbA1c of 6.5 percent or higher, a fasting plasma glucose of 126 mg/dL or higher, or a two-hour glucose of 200 mg/dL or higher after a glucose load, normally confirmed by a repeat test. Prediabetes is an HbA1c of 5.7 to 6.4 percent or a fasting glucose of 100 to 125 mg/dL. A common HbA1c goal for many non-pregnant adults with diabetes is below 7 percent, but goals are individualised by the care team.

A rapid fall in glucose, for example more than 2 mg/dL per minute on a continuous glucose monitor, suggests a low may follow soon. Eating, exercise, illness, stress and insulin timing all affect glucose. Medicine and insulin doses must only be changed on the advice of the treating doctor.
